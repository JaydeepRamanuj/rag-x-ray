# The Stages of a Modern AI Pipeline: From Raw Data to a Running Application

"AI pipeline" means two different things depending on who says it. To a model lab, it is the path from raw data to a trained model. To an application developer, it is the path from a user's request to an answer, built on top of someone else's model. This document covers both, because an engineer working with LLMs today needs a working picture of the whole chain: where models come from, how they are served, and how applications wrap them.

## Part 1: Building the model

### Stage 1: Data collection and curation

Pretraining data for a frontier model is measured in **trillions of tokens**. The main sources are web crawls (Common Crawl is the usual starting point), code repositories, books, scientific papers, encyclopedias, and increasingly **synthetic data** generated by earlier models.

Raw web data is mostly unusable, so curation is a large part of the work:

- **Extraction:** pull clean text out of HTML, dropping menus, ads and boilerplate.
- **Language identification** and filtering to the languages wanted.
- **Quality filtering:** heuristics (length, symbol ratios, repeated lines) and **classifier filters** trained to recognize educational or high-quality text. Hugging Face's FineWeb-Edu dataset, for example, used an LLM-labelled classifier to keep educational pages.
- **Deduplication:** exact and near-duplicate removal (often with MinHash). Duplicated text wastes compute and encourages memorization.
- **Safety and privacy filtering:** removing personal information, toxic content and known benchmark test sets (to avoid **contamination**, where a model scores well on a test because it saw the answers).
- **Data mixing:** choosing the proportions of web text, code, math and other domains. The mix strongly shapes what the model is good at.

### Stage 2: Tokenizer training

A tokenizer is trained on a sample of the corpus, usually with **byte-pair encoding**, to choose the vocabulary. This happens before model training and cannot change afterwards without retraining, because every embedding row belongs to one vocabulary entry.

### Stage 3: Pretraining

The model, a decoder-only Transformer for almost all current LLMs, is trained to predict the next token across the whole corpus. Key engineering pieces:

- **Hardware:** thousands to tens of thousands of GPUs (NVIDIA H100, H200 and Blackwell-generation parts) or Google **TPUs**, connected with high-bandwidth networking.
- **Parallelism:** the model and data are split across devices in several ways at once. **Data parallelism** gives each device different examples; **tensor parallelism** splits individual weight matrices; **pipeline parallelism** puts different layers on different devices; **expert parallelism** spreads mixture-of-experts experts across devices. Techniques such as ZeRO and FSDP shard optimizer state to save memory.
- **Mixed precision:** computing in 16-bit (bf16) or even 8-bit (FP8) formats instead of 32-bit to double or quadruple throughput.
- **Stability:** loss spikes and hardware failures are routine at this scale, so training checkpoints regularly and restarts from the last good point.

Pretraining is the most expensive stage by far, costing tens to hundreds of millions of dollars for frontier models. It is often followed by **mid-training** or annealing: a shorter phase on higher-quality, longer-context, code- or math-heavy data, sometimes extending the context window.

### Stage 4: Post-training

Post-training turns the base model into a useful, safe assistant:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT):** train on curated example conversations, including tool use and refusals.
2. **Preference optimization:** RLHF with a reward model, or DPO directly on preference pairs, to make answers more helpful and better formatted. Anthropic's **Constitutional AI** (2022) replaced much of the human labelling with model feedback guided by a written set of principles, an approach known as **RLAIF**.
3. **Reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks:** math problems with checkable answers, code with unit tests, and tool-use tasks with checkable outcomes. This produces **reasoning models** that spend more tokens thinking before they answer, trading inference cost for accuracy (**test-time compute**).
4. **Safety training** and **red-teaming:** testing for and training against harmful outputs, jailbreaks and misuse.

### Stage 5: Evaluation

Models are evaluated before release on public benchmarks and internal tests. Well-known benchmarks include **MMLU** (multiple-choice knowledge across 57 subjects), **GPQA** (graduate-level science questions), **SWE-bench** (fixing real GitHub issues), **HumanEval** (writing Python functions), **AIME** (competition math) and **Humanity's Last Exam**. Popular benchmarks saturate quickly and can be contaminated, so labs rely on private evals and human preference comparisons such as **LMArena** (formerly Chatbot Arena), where people vote between two anonymous models' answers.

### Stage 6: Compression and distillation

Large models are often used to make smaller ones:

- **Distillation:** train a small "student" model on the outputs (or probability distributions) of a large "teacher". Many small open models are distilled from larger siblings.
- **Quantization:** store weights in 8-bit or 4-bit integers instead of 16-bit floats. A 70-billion-parameter model needs about 140 GB at 16-bit but about 35–40 GB at 4-bit, with a modest quality loss. Formats include GPTQ, AWQ and GGUF (used by llama.cpp and Ollama).
- **Pruning:** remove weights or whole layers that contribute little.

### Stage 7: Release

Models ship either as a **hosted API** (closed-weight models such as GPT, Claude and Gemini) or as **open-weight** downloads (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma, gpt-oss) under licences that vary in how permissive they are. Release usually comes with a **model card** describing training, intended use, evaluations and limitations.

## Part 2: Serving the model (inference)

### Inference engines

Running a model efficiently is its own discipline. Inference engines such as **vLLM**, **SGLang**, **TensorRT-LLM** and **llama.cpp** (for CPUs and laptops) provide:

- **Continuous batching:** many users' requests share each GPU step; new requests join as others finish, instead of waiting for a whole batch to complete.
- **KV cache management:** PagedAttention (from vLLM) stores the cache in fixed-size blocks like operating-system memory pages, avoiding fragmentation and allowing shared prefixes.
- **Prefix (prompt) caching:** reuse the computed cache for identical prompt prefixes across requests.
- **Speculative decoding:** a small draft model guesses several tokens ahead and the large model verifies them in one pass, producing the same output faster.
- **Quantized kernels** for 8-bit and 4-bit weights.

### Serving metrics

- **TTFT (time to first token):** how long until the first output token, dominated by queueing and prefill.
- **TPOT / ITL (time per output token, inter-token latency):** the decode speed users feel while text streams.
- **Throughput:** total tokens per second across all users, which sets cost per token.
- **Cost per million tokens**, priced separately for input, cached input and output tokens.

There is a trade-off between latency for one user and throughput for all users: larger batches are cheaper per token but slower per request.

### API gateways and routing

In front of the models sit API gateways that handle authentication, **rate limits** (requests per minute, tokens per minute, requests per day), quotas, retries and failover between providers. Routers such as OpenRouter or LiteLLM give one API over many models and can choose a cheaper model for easy requests and a stronger one for hard requests.

## Part 3: The application pipeline

This is where most developers work. A request from a user passes through several stages, each one a place where quality, cost and latency are decided.

### Stage 1: Input handling and guardrails

Validate and limit the input (length caps, rate limits per user), and check it for abuse or **prompt injection**: text that tries to override the system's instructions, which may arrive directly from the user or indirectly through a retrieved document, web page or email. Input classifiers and strict separation of instructions from data reduce this risk; no current technique removes it entirely.

### Stage 2: Context engineering

**Context engineering** is the job of deciding exactly what goes into the model's context window for each call. It covers:

- **The system prompt:** role, rules, output format, examples.
- **Retrieval (RAG):** the most relevant passages from documents, found by vector, keyword or hybrid search and reranked.
- **Memory:** conversation history, summarized when it grows long, and longer-term facts about the user.
- **Tool definitions:** names, descriptions and JSON schemas for the functions the model may call.
- **Structured data:** records from databases or APIs relevant to the request.

The term became popular in 2025 as people noticed that most agent failures were context failures (missing information, too much noise, stale or conflicting instructions) rather than model failures. The window is a limited budget: every token costs money and latency, and irrelevant tokens can lower answer quality.

### Stage 3: Model call

Choose the model (cost, speed, quality, context size), set parameters (temperature, maximum output tokens, reasoning effort), request **structured output** when code will consume the result (JSON mode or a JSON Schema the provider enforces), and stream the response for interactive use. Handle errors: retry **429 (rate limited)** and 5xx responses with exponential backoff, respecting the `retry-after` header, and fall back to another model or provider if one is down.

### Stage 4: Tool calling and agent loops

When tools are available, the model may reply with a **tool call** (a function name and JSON arguments) instead of text. The application runs the function, adds the result to the conversation as a tool message, and calls the model again. An **agent** repeats this loop until the task is done or a step limit is reached. Agent pipelines need step limits, cost budgets, permission checks on side-effecting tools, and human approval for risky actions.

### Stage 5: Output handling

Validate structured output against its schema (with Pydantic or Zod) and retry or repair on failure. Check citations point to real sources. Run output guardrails for safety, personal data and policy. Render Markdown, code and citations for the user.

### Stage 6: Caching

Caching appears at several levels: **embedding caches** keyed by a hash of the text (so the same chunk is never embedded twice), **retrieval and rerank caches** keyed by query and settings, provider **prompt caching** for long stable prefixes, and sometimes **semantic caches** that return a stored answer for a question very similar to an earlier one.

### Stage 7: Observability

Log every step: the full prompt, retrieved chunks with scores, model, parameters, input and output token counts, latency per stage, cost, errors and retries. Traces make it possible to answer "why did it say that?" and "where did the time and money go?". Common tools include LangSmith, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix and OpenTelemetry-based tracing.

### Stage 8: Evaluation and the feedback loop

Production AI systems need **evals** the way traditional software needs tests:

- **Offline evals:** a fixed set of questions with expected answers or source passages, scored on every change. For RAG that means retrieval metrics (hit rate@k, MRR) and generation metrics (faithfulness, answer relevance), often graded by an **LLM-as-a-judge**.
- **Online signals:** user feedback (thumbs up or down), whether users retry or rephrase, and sampled human review.
- **Regression testing:** run the eval set before shipping a new prompt, model or retrieval setting, and compare with the previous run.

Failures found in production become new eval items, so the eval set grows with the product. Teams that change one setting at a time and measure the effect improve steadily; teams that change prompts by feel tend to fix one case and break three others.

## How the parts connect

| Layer                    | Who builds it                              | Main concerns                                |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Data and pretraining     | Model labs                                 | Data quality, compute, scaling               |
| Post-training and safety | Model labs                                 | Helpfulness, reasoning, harmlessness         |
| Inference and serving    | Labs, cloud providers, inference companies | Latency, throughput, cost per token          |
| Gateways and routing     | Platform teams                             | Rate limits, failover, model choice          |
| Application pipeline     | Product engineers                          | Context, retrieval, tools, guardrails, evals |

An application engineer rarely trains a model, but knowledge of the lower layers explains the behaviour seen at the top: why output tokens cost more than input tokens (decode is memory-bound), why long prompts are slow (prefill and quadratic attention), why caching a prefix is cheap (the KV cache), why models invent facts (next-token training on a fixed corpus), and why retrieval and evals are the developer's main levers on quality.
